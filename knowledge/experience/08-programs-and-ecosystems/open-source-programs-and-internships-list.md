# Why this points you to a living list instead of fixed dates

Program dates, stipend amounts and eligibility rules change almost every year, and a knowledge file that hardcodes this year's exact dates becomes wrong the moment next year's cycle opens. Instead of copying exact days here, this file names the real programs worth knowing about and describes their general shape and rhythm. For the current, verified dates and amounts, check the community-maintained list at github.com/yashisrani/List-of-OpenSource-Programs (also published as a website), which its own maintainers say they re-verify monthly, and always cross-check against the program's own official page before making real plans around a date.

# Paid programs worth knowing about

**Google Summer of Code (GSoC)**, run by Google, is the best known: contributors work on an accepted organization's project over roughly three to five months with a real stipend, typically somewhere between $1,500 and $6,600 depending on the project's length (a 12 to 22 week range), generally following a yearly cycle of organizations applying near the start of the year, contributors applying a couple of months later, and coding running through the middle of the year into early winter.

**Outreachy**, run by Software Freedom Conservancy, specifically supports people from groups underrepresented in tech, pays a $7,000 stipend, and runs two cohorts a year, roughly one starting in early summer and one starting in winter.

**LFX Mentorship**, run by the Linux Foundation, covers many CNCF and Linux Foundation projects (including Kubernetes, Kubeflow and others referenced elsewhere in this knowledge base), typically pays between $1,000 and $6,600, and runs multiple separate terms across the year rather than just one annual cycle, so new rounds open repeatedly. The closely related **LF Decentralized Trust Mentorship** and **Open Mainframe Project Mentorship** follow the same LFX-style terms and a broadly similar stipend range.

**MLH Fellowship** (Major League Hacking) is a 12-week remote program with multiple cohorts and tracks a year, aimed at students and recent graduates, providing an educational stipend rather than a fixed public dollar figure.

Other real, paid options worth knowing about: **Igalia Coding Experience** pays $7,000; **Summer of Bitcoin** pays around $3,000, in Bitcoin; **FOSSEE Summer Fellowship**, run by IIT Bombay, is paid and mainly relevant if you are in India; **Code for GovTech's Dedicated Mentoring Program**, also India-based and focused on government technology, pays roughly ₹1,00,000; and the **European Summer of Code (ESoC)** and **Open Source Research Experience (OSRE)** both offer stipends or sponsored opportunities that vary by project rather than one fixed figure. Every amount here changes from year to year and should always be confirmed on the program's own official page before you rely on it.

# Certificate and recognition programs, without a stipend

Not every worthwhile program pays money, and some are excellent low-stakes ways to make your very first contributions. **Hacktoberfest**, run every October by DigitalOcean, rewards any accepted pull requests during the month with digital recognition, not money. **24 Pull Requests** runs every December with a similar spirit. **Season of KDE** offers a certificate and swag, not a stipend. Various regional "Winter of Code" programs (such as KWOC, DWoC and JWOC, mostly organized by Indian student communities) work the same way: certificates and mentored, structured first contributions, without a stipend and without requiring you to already be an expert.

A number of other well-known mentorship-style programs are also unpaid or community-driven rather than stipend-based, and it is worth not assuming otherwise: the **Free Software Foundation Internship**, **Swift Mentorship Program**, **KubeStellar IFoS (Interns for Open Source)**, **Kubernetes Release Shadow Program**, **Linux Kernel Mentorship Program**, and **Nexus Spring of Code (NSoC)** are all genuinely valuable for the experience, mentorship and portfolio they provide, but none of them pay a stipend. A few others, such as **Djangonaut Space** (free, mentorship-based), **sktime Mentoring Program**, and the **FOSSASIA Internship**, vary project to project and are sometimes paid depending on the specific round, so check the current round's own page rather than assuming either way.

# Where to actually find beginner-friendly issues

Beyond just browsing a project's own issue tracker, a few maintained tools exist specifically to surface approachable issues across many projects at once: **Up For Grabs**, **First Timers Only**, **CodeTriage** (which emails you a small number of issues regularly instead of overwhelming you), and **contrib.rocks** (which lets you filter by technology and difficulty). On GitHub itself, searching for the labels `good first issue`, `help wanted`, `beginner`, `documentation`, and `hacktoberfest` (during October) across repositories is a reliable way to find approachable, intentionally-flagged tasks.

# The rough yearly rhythm, without exact dates

Roughly: GSoC's organization applications open early in the year, contributor applications follow about two months later, and the actual coding period runs from early summer through the following winter with a mid-point evaluation partway through. LFX Mentorship does not wait for a single yearly window; it opens new terms multiple times a year, so it is always worth checking what is currently open. Outreachy's two cohorts roughly split the year into two intake windows. Hacktoberfest is always in October, and 24 Pull Requests is always in December, making both reliable, low-commitment ways to build a first contribution habit every year regardless of what else is or is not currently open.

Always verify exact dates, stipend amounts and eligibility on the program's own official website before committing time, since every detail above is a general pattern, not a guarantee for any specific year.
