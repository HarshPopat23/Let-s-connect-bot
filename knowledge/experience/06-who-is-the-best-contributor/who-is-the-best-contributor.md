# It is not the person with the most pull requests

It is easy to assume the best contributor is whoever has opened the most pull requests, or closed the most issues. That is not actually true. Someone can open a huge number of small pull requests and still not be the person a community is genuinely glad to have around. Counting pull requests and issues measures activity, not the actual value someone brings to a project.

# What actually makes someone the best contributor

The best contributor is someone people genuinely enjoy working with. They treat the codebase and the community around it almost like family, not just a place to collect contributions. A few concrete things this looks like in practice:

They **welcome new contributors** instead of ignoring them or making them feel like an outsider. They make sure that no new contributor struggles to find a way to get involved, actively pointing people toward good starting tasks instead of leaving them to figure it out completely alone.

They **answer other people's doubts on the community chat**, such as Slack, even when the question is not directly about their own work. This alone makes a maintainer's job noticeably easier, since every question answered by a community member is one less question the maintainer has to personally handle.

They **review other people's pull requests**, not only submit their own. A community where everyone only opens pull requests and nobody reviews anyone else's quickly becomes bottlenecked on whichever maintainer is left to review everything alone.

They **show up in meetings with real ideas**, proposing new directions or improvements that could genuinely help the project grow, not just reporting on tasks they finished. This kind of thinking, about where the project could go next, is exactly what can meaningfully accelerate a project, and it is a different skill from simply writing code.

Put together, this is why contribution never really means just opening issues and pull requests. It means actually participating in the life of the project.

# Two habits worth calling out specifically

**Do not send maintainers direct, private messages** to ask your questions. Ask in the project's public channels instead, such the relevant Slack channel, GitHub discussion, or the issue itself. This is not just etiquette: a public question and its answer help every future person who has the same doubt, while a private message only helps you, and it quietly adds to a maintainer's private workload instead of the shared community's workload.

**Build in public.** Whatever you are working on, your progress, your doubts, your half-finished ideas, share it in the open rather than working on it quietly and only showing up once it is finished. This is exactly the same principle as the first habit: it turns your individual work into something the whole community can see, learn from, and jump in on, instead of a private effort only you benefit from.
