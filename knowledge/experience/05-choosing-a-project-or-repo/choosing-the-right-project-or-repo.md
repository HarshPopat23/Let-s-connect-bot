# Two different mindsets, depending on your actual goal

There is not one single correct way to pick a project. It genuinely depends on why you are contributing in the first place, and there are two different mindsets worth separating clearly.

**Mindset one: you want to contribute for what contributing itself gives you**, not specifically to get into a paid program. This is a completely valid goal on its own, not a lesser one; see "why open source is worth far more than any single program" elsewhere in this knowledge base for exactly why. In this case, pick a project in a language or framework you already know. Working in a technology you are comfortable with means you can focus your energy on understanding the project itself, instead of also fighting the basics of a new language at the same time.

**Mindset two: you specifically want to get into a program like GSoC or LFX Mentorship.** Here, your programming language or framework matters much less than it feels like it should. The actual skills these programs are testing for, reading someone else's code, finding a real problem, communicating clearly, following through on a task, stay the same no matter what language the project happens to be written in. Instead of picking by tech stack, it is more useful to pick by studying the organization and the program itself, which the next section explains.

# Reading the room inside an organization with multiple projects

Many organizations that take part in mentorship programs offer several separate projects at once, not just one. Before picking one, it is worth actually looking at the signals available to you.

Check the commit activity on each project: how many commits exist, and who is making most of them. If one particular project already has one contributor clearly doing the majority of the work, treat that project as effectively already spoken for; your odds there are lower simply because someone already has a head start and a track record with the maintainers. If an organization is offering, say, five projects and one of them is dominated like this, that still leaves the other four, where the field is much more open for you to establish yourself.

Also check the organization's community chat, such as Slack or Discord. If genuinely nobody is active there, that is an ambiguous signal and worth digging into further rather than guessing: it could mean people are already quietly settled into that project and simply not chatting about it in public, or it could mean nobody has actually shown up for that project yet, meaning you would be arriving early with a real opportunity. If people are actively there instead, try to get yourself associated with a specific project for that mentorship term as early as you reasonably can, through real contributions, so that by the time applications open, you already have a visible history there.

This is honestly the social and strategic side of picking a project, not the purely technical side, and it is worth being upfront about that rather than pretending only code quality decides these outcomes.

# If you are a genuine beginner and it is not application season yet

When GSoC or LFX applications are not even open yet, and you are still early in your journey, a good strategy is to pick smaller, manageable projects that belong to organizations with a strong track record of coming back to these programs year after year. That way, by the time the next cycle actually opens, you already have real history and visible contributions with that organization, instead of starting from zero.

To find organizations like this, read the past program data directly instead of guessing:

- For GSoC, past participating organizations across previous years are listed at gsocorganizations.dev.
- For LFX Mentorship in general, the official mentorship portal is at lfx.linuxfoundation.org/tools/mentorship/.
- For CNCF's specific LFX mentorship cohorts, past projects are tracked directly in the github.com/cncf/mentoring repository, for example under a path like programs/lfx-mentorship/2026 for that year's cohort.

Reading a few past cohorts from these sources tells you which organizations reliably return, what kind of projects they tend to offer, and roughly what skill level their past mentees started at, which is far more useful for planning ahead than picking a project at random.
