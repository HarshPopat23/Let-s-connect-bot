# Understand why before how

When you pick an issue, first understand why the problem matters. Reproduce it, identify who is affected, learn the expected behavior and locate the smallest relevant part of the codebase. You do not need to understand an entire mature repository before starting.

Explore the issue-related files and trace how data or control moves through them. Write down the files, functions, assumptions and questions. Solving several related issues over time creates a real mental model of the codebase.

# Use AI as a guide, not an invisible author

AI can explain terminology, locate likely files, summarize unfamiliar code, propose test cases and help interpret errors. You must validate everything and understand every submitted line. Start by forming your own explanation and plan. Compare AI suggestions against repository code, documentation and tests.

Do not submit AI output directly. Maintainers quickly notice generic issue descriptions, irrelevant changes, invented APIs and comments that the contributor cannot defend. The contributor remains responsible for correctness, licensing, security and communication.

# Quality over contribution count

Pull requests vary widely. A documentation correction may take minutes; a design or bug fix may take days or months. Count alone does not measure impact. Prefer work that solves a real maintainer or user problem, includes tests and fits project priorities.

Old issues can be valuable, but ask whether they are still wanted before investing heavily. Some remain open because priorities changed or the proposed work is no longer desirable.

# Reviews and waiting

A pull request does not need to be merged immediately to demonstrate learning. Constructive review conversations, responsive updates and thoughtful technical decisions are meaningful signals.

When a review is delayed, check automated tests, review your own diff, improve documentation, test edge cases, help another contributor or work on an unrelated task. After a reasonable wait, send one concise follow-up in the project's preferred channel. Do not repeatedly tag several maintainers.

# Programs are not the purpose of open source

GSoC, LFX and Outreachy can provide structured mentorship, but contribution should not begin and end with selection season. Contributions do not guarantee selection. A strong applicant understands the project, participates professionally, writes an original proposal and shows the ability to complete relevant work.

Continue contributing when an application is rejected if the project and community remain valuable to you. Rejection can result from limited slots, project priority, proposal fit, eligibility or competition and is not a complete judgment of your ability.
